Question A: customer and transaction data contains PII and is shared
between internal platforms and an external partner. design the controls
from ingestion through consumption, connecting technical controls to
trust and compliance rather than treating governance as a separate
governance-team responsibility.

my starting position on this: if governance only lives in a policy doc
or a separate team's dashboard, it's not actually controlling anything,
it's documenting what happened after the fact. the controls have to be
in the pipeline itself, enforced by the platform, not by someone
reviewing a spreadsheet once a quarter. so I'd build this in layers,
ingestion, storage, processing, consumption, sharing, and put an
enforcement point at each one instead of one big gate at the end.

INGESTION
PII gets identified and tagged at the point it lands, not discovered
later. that means classifying columns (name, account number, transaction
detail) as part of the Bronze schema itself, using column-level tags in
Unity Catalog (or whatever catalog layer sits under the lakehouse), so
"this column is PII" is metadata the platform knows, not something a
downstream analyst has to remember. anything landing without expected
classification gets flagged, same idea as the contract violation
handling in the pipeline design, an unexpected sensitive field showing
up is a signal, not something that silently flows through.

ACCESS CONTROL
access is role and purpose based, enforced by the catalog, not by
"please don't query that table." row and column level security so a
support analyst can see enough to do their job without seeing full card
numbers, and a data scientist training a model gets aggregated or masked
data by default, full PII access is the exception that requires
justification, not the default. service principals for pipelines,
individual identities for people, so every access is attributable to
something real, not a shared credential nobody can trace back.

MASKING AND DE-IDENTIFICATION
raw PII doesn't flow past where it's actually needed. dynamic views or
column masking functions applied at the catalog layer so the same
physical table serves a masked version to most consumers and the raw
version only to roles that are explicitly cleared, tokenization for
things like account numbers where downstream systems need a stable
identifier but not the real value. this is the difference between "we
have a policy against misuse" and "misuse isn't possible with the access
most people actually have."

ENCRYPTION
encrypted at rest and in transit as a baseline, that part's table
stakes. on top of that, field-level encryption for the highest
sensitivity data, so even someone with storage-level access without the
right key can't read it in the clear. keys managed and rotated outside
the data platform itself, so a compromise of the lakehouse isn't
automatically a compromise of the encryption.

EXTERNAL PARTNER SHARING
this is the part I'd be most careful about, since once data leaves the
platform I lose most of my controls. so I don't send data out, I share a
scoped view of it, Delta Sharing or equivalent, a defined subset of
columns and rows the partner is actually contracted to see, masked or
tokenized where the partner doesn't need the raw value, and that scope
is versioned and reviewed the same way a data contract is, changes to
what's shared go through the same rigor as changes to schema. audit logs
on the share itself, so "what did the partner actually pull and when"
is answerable without asking the partner.

AUDIT AND LINEAGE
every read and write is logged with who, what, and when, at the catalog
level, not bolted on by each team separately. lineage tracked from
ingestion through to whatever report or model consumed the data, so if
there's ever a "how did this PII end up here" question, the answer is a
lineage graph, not a week of interviews.

RETENTION AND DELETION
retention rules enforced by the pipeline, not remembered by a person,
data past its retention window gets purged or archived automatically,
and if there's a deletion request tied to a specific customer, that has
to be something the pipeline can actually execute against the business
key, across every table it landed in, not just the one obvious table.

MONITORING
same alerting philosophy as the pipeline design, unusual access patterns
(a service account suddenly pulling a full table instead of its normal
scoped query, a spike in raw-PII reads) get flagged the same way a
duplicate-rate spike or SLA miss would, this is a signal worth paging
someone on, not a monthly report someone skims.

why this connects to trust and compliance directly: an auditor or a
partner doesn't trust a policy document, they trust that the system
enforces what the policy says. if access control, masking, and
encryption are built into the catalog and the pipeline, then compliance
isn't a separate checklist someone runs before a review, it's just how
the data moves, which is the only version of this that actually holds up
when someone's credentials get phished or a new engineer ships a query
that shouldn't work, the platform stops it either way, whether or not
anyone from governance is watching that day.
